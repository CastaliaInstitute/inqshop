Garage Schooling · homeschool with sleeves rolled up

Serious making deserves a serious curriculum—not just a pile of kits

Garage Schooling is what we call inquiry that lives in a real workshop: cardboard to combustion, paint to programming, with artifacts and notebooks that prove the work. iNQshop is the public curriculum map for that life—especially for dads building culture on Saturday, not only delivering worksheets Monday.

This page is intentionally about what you teach and how it ladders from about age two through the teen years (and beyond if you want). Bench heights, dust collection, laser bays, and phased construction live on the companion site garage.castalia.institute—so you can point a contractor (or your future self) at one URL for the room, and this URL for the schooling.

Why Garage Schooling

Most burnout is not “too little curriculum.” It is nowhere for serious making to live. When the dining table is the lab, projects shrink to what you can clear before dinner. Garage Schooling assumes you are fighting for a room where mess, noise, and long-running builds are normal—then asks what intellectual habits should grow in that place.

  • Presence that scales. Fifteen honest minutes at the bench beats an hour of nagging about seatwork.
  • Proof for transcripts and life. Rigs, notebooks, teardown photos, and rebuilt carburetors are evidence—not vibes.
  • Siblings together. Wonder-stage beside Seed-stage: same room culture, different duties.
  • Dads and hands-on parents. A garage curriculum is a concrete way to lead formation—not only logistics.

What we mean by “Garage Schooling”

Not a brand of worksheets—a stance: your family treats the garage (or shed, or dedicated bay) as a legitimate school site where bodies and materials do real work. Reading still happens at the table; here we privilege measurement, fabrication, engines, failure, and repair. The goal is not vocational tracking for toddlers—it is competence with consequence, paced to age and supervision.

Habits we repeat on purpose

  • Name before use — tool, chemical, machine part.
  • Notebook adjacent — even a labeled ziplock of sketches counts at Wonder.
  • Cleanup is closure — reset the shop so courage returns next time.
  • Explain the failure — especially when smoke was optional.

Strands (ages 2+ through advanced)

Same project families at increasing depth. PreK lives in Wonder (~ages 2–4, always with a hand nearby). The rhythm stays observe → make → measure → model → explain → publish → teach. Engines & propulsion sit alongside form, motion, light, matter, life, and civilization—because power and thermodynamics belong in a complete technical education, not only in a textbook margin.

Core outputs at every level

  • Wonder (2–4) — one kept artifact, photo, or “tell the camera” clip
  • Artifact — the built or repaired thing
  • Notebook — sketches, torque specs, mixture notes, reflections
  • Model — CAD, diagram, or theory sketch
  • Demonstration — it runs, it carries load, it stops safely
  • Explanation — oral or written defense (why it failed counts)
  • Publication — portfolio, guide for a sibling, short film
  • Teaching — hand knowledge downward

PreK in the workshop (age 2+)

Two-year-olds share the same culture: short invitations, parallel work with an adult, cleanup as pride. Wet and messy stay on the “soft” side of the room; running engines and sharp perimeter tools stay off or behind a hard rule you never improvise. For where those zones go in the floor plan, see the Garage layout guide.

Starter rhythm (~15–20 min)

  1. Arrive — smock, name one tool.
  2. One invitation — tape strips, big brush stroke, sort bolts by size.
  3. Make — narrate; quit when attention drifts.
  4. Keep one thing — display rail or photo.
  5. Reset — “shop ready for next time.”

Safety (2–4)

  • Choking hazards controlled; no loose magnets or batteries in reach.
  • Fuels and solvents locked; child-grade media only in the bay.
  • Toddlers upwind of dust and fumes; engines only with door policy you write down.
  • Hot glue, iron, drill: adult-only; offer safe substitutes.

Capabilities this curriculum expects (engines highlighted)

Garage Schooling is honest about power. By mid-Builder many students can participate in supervised small-engine maintenance; by Studio they should meet written shop rules the way they meet algebra axioms. The physical zones page suggests how to separate toddler paint from a teardown bay—here we list intellectual and manual capabilities the strands develop.

Capability band Examples in the garage
Engines & thermal systems Two- and four-stroke cycle literacy; mixture and load; cooling and lubrication; safe fuel handling; compression testing conceptually then with a gauge; alternators/motors as paired machines
Mechanical power transmission Belt vs chain; gear ratios; torque multiplication; why a clutch exists
Electrical power Volts/amps/hours; fuses; motor controllers; basic wiring with strain relief and respect for insulation
Measurement & honesty Calipers, torque wrench, timing marks, notebook columns for “predicted vs observed”
Digital fabrication CAM toolpaths tied to material limits; laser parameters with fire sense; printer maintenance as character lesson
Wood & structures Grain direction, joinery that carries load, fastener schedules
Wet & craft media Color chemistry at an age-appropriate depth; archival habits for portfolios
